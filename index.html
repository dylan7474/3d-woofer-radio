3D Woofer Radio

A compact, battery-powered radio with a woofer in the centre, a mid-range speaker either side and a tweeter at each end.

Design stage. Nothing is built, printed or tested yet. The numbers here are first-pass estimates from published speaker data. Next: the 3D model.

The idea

Inspired by the look and layout of the 1980s "3D Super Woofer" boomboxes, but not a clone: our own size and parts, with no tape decks or CD. A wide, low, rectangular cabinet with a black metal speaker grille across the front, each speaker sitting in its own pod (a recessed section of the grille, with its own sealed chamber behind). As on the original, the pods sit close together across the front, not spread out. The two vertical tweeters mark the ends of the cabinet, one at each end. There's also a built-in rechargeable battery. A "luxury" build that uses parts a mass producer would probably skip.

Speaker pods: first-pass sums

  • Mid pods: with the woofer taking over at about 130–150 Hz, a sealed pod of about 2–3 L per FR 10 is enough (roll-off roughly 118–133 Hz).
  • Woofer pod (decided): 3 L sealed. For the DC130B-4 that gives a smooth response (Qtc about 0.79) with the −3 dB point near 104 Hz. A bigger pod barely moves that point (4 L: 103 Hz, 6 L: 105 Hz), and 2.2 L saves only about 5 cm of depth at the cost of a small bump near 130 Hz. About 16–20 cm deep, in line with the mid pods, so the whole cabinet can be roughly 20 cm deep. A ported pod would reach about 58 Hz but needs about 9 L (over 50 cm deep at this width), so it is ruled out for a compact radio.
  • Size: roughly 480 mm wide for all five pods side by side, and at least 155 mm high (first-pass, from the real frame sizes).

These come from the standard sealed-box formula and published speaker parameters. Real enclosures and drivers will differ, so the plan is to build and measure.

Cheapest route is roughly £25 (amp, DAC, fixed supply); about £50 with the variable bench supply. The AIYIMA board's "USB" input only plays from a USB stick, so it is not a DAC and we still need the PCM5102 board. The listing's pot descriptions sit under the Bluetooth version; the non-Bluetooth photo shows the same five knobs, but that is not explicitly confirmed.

The test should answer: (1) is idle hiss and power-up pop acceptable? (2) does the bass knob hand over cleanly to the mids at about 130–150 Hz? (3) does the amp stay happy with the Mini-8 and its filter next to a mid? (4) does it work at 9–12.6 V? (5) what is the idle current?

Things to check

  • Not every "2.1 TPA3116" board has a working crossover: buy one whose listing names the low-pass control.
  • The sub output is rated 100 W into 2 Ω; a 4 Ω woofer gets less, and 12 V gives less again, though still plenty for a 5″ woofer in a small box.
  • The 8 Ω Mini-8 wired in parallel with a 4 Ω mid could present as low as about 2.7 Ω where both play, below the amp's 4 Ω rating. A series capacitor keeps the tweeter quiet at low frequencies and the mid's impedance rises at the top, so the real load should stay higher, but this must be measured before trusting it.
  • Dayton gives no recommended crossover for the Mini-8 (usable range from about 3.5–4 kHz up). Builders cross it around 5–6 kHz, and one suggests using it as a blended super-tweeter above about 10 kHz with a gentle slope. Plan: a first-order high-pass of roughly 6 kHz (a capacitor of about 3.3 µF), then adjust by ear and measurement.
  • Battery safety, amp ventilation and cabinet weight.

Next steps

  1. 3D model of the cabinet and pods (next): a rectangular cabinet about 480 mm wide, at least 155 mm high and about 20 cm deep, with five tightly packed pods behind a black metal grille (vertical slots for the tweeters), checked against the real parts before any printing.
  2. Bench-test the amp (see above) before committing to the cabinet.
  3. Design the Mini-8 high-pass filter (start near 6 kHz, about 3.3 µF) and tune it by ear and measurement.
  4. Choose the battery pack and charging board.
